When a moat is written by a regulator

The rule
was the moat

FICO lost more than half its value in two months. Its scores didn't get worse and its revenue kept growing. What changed was the rule that forced mortgage lenders to buy them. This is how that happened, what could come next, and the seven checks I now run so I don't get caught by the same pattern.

The mechanism

Why a $0.99 rival is a big deal

For years, loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac had to carry a Classic FICO score. Lenders had no choice, so FICO could raise its mortgage price again and again. Reports on the latest quarter note that mortgage growth came from price, not from more loans.

Once one pricing table accepts either score, a lender who picks VantageScore gets the same result for a fraction of the cost. The chart below shows what that gap looks like on a single mortgage application.

Score cost for one borrower whose loan closesIllustration · 3 scores (one per bureau) · list prices

Traditional model: $10 per score. Performance model: $4.95 per score plus $33 per closed loan. FICO 10T: $0.99 per score plus $65 per closed loan. VantageScore 4.0: $0.99 per score, and TransUnion gives it away free through 2026. Resellers add their own fees on top.

What happens next

Three futures for FICO

Almost everything depends on one line of the income statement. Last quarter, mortgage origination scores brought in $283M, 71% of score sales to lenders and about 42% of all revenue. They cost almost nothing to produce, so they carry most of the profit.

My read. Weighting the three cases 25 / 50 / 25 gives about $575, a little below its $617 close on 29 Sep. The market isn't obviously wrong, and there is no margin of safety yet. FICO also fails every check in the stress test below, so I'm watching, not buying, until the November guidance.

Takeaways

Seven lessons I keep

  1. Ask who grants the moat.

    A moat that comes from a rule can be taken away by a rule. Before paying up, ask: if the rule disappeared tomorrow, would customers still choose this product?

    FICO passes in credit cards and auto loans, where lenders choose it freely. It fails in mortgages, where a rule did the choosing.

  2. Growth from price has a political ceiling.

    Check how much growth comes from raising prices versus selling more. If those price increases land on a bill voters already complain about, expect someone in government to notice.

    Mortgage score prices rose sharply during a national argument about housing affordability. The regulator then named FICO's pricing as the problem.

  3. Watch when your distributor owns your rival.

    If the companies that sell your product also own a competing one, they can switch their effort the moment the rules allow it.

    The three credit bureaus sold FICO scores and co-own VantageScore. TransUnion made VantageScore free, and its share of their mortgage pulls went from under 5% to about 30%.

  4. A premium multiple doubles the fall.

    At 40–50x earnings, a stock is priced for years of unchallenged pricing power. When that is questioned, earnings estimates and the multiple drop at the same time.

    The multiple went from 50x+ to about 15x while revenue was still growing 26%.

  5. Cheap versus its own history is not a floor.

    Historical multiples describe the old business. Once the business model changes, "lowest in ten years" says little about where the bottom is.

    In March 2026, 24x was called a 10-year low, with an average target near $2,068 and the stock around $1,100. It has since fallen about another 40%.

  6. A thesis-breaking gap means exit, not add.

    A big one-day drop caused by the market is noise. A big drop caused by news that breaks the thesis is information. Reduce or exit, and buy back only on new evidence.

    Each −17% day was the regulator taking another step in the same direction. Averaging down turned each step into a bigger loss.

  7. Debt-funded buybacks at the top turn a setback into a squeeze.

    A company that borrows to buy back its own stock at a premium price is betting that nothing changes. If the business then weakens, the debt stays while the shares it bought are worth half.

    In the June quarter FICO spent $1.96B buying its own stock at an average $1,149, partly with a new $1.5B loan. Debt is now $5.6B, about 3.7x a year of operating profit, with the stock at $617.

The check

Moat stress test

Run this before adding any "wide moat" name, and again when a position gaps down 15% or more on company news.

#QuestionFICO, mortgage
01Would customers still choose it if no rule required it?No
02Is most recent growth from price rather than volume, on a politically visible bill?Yes
03Does a distributor or partner own a competing product?Yes
04Is the multiple well above the market (35x+)?Yes
05Has a regulator or politician named the company's pricing in public?Yes
06Is there a written exit rule for a thesis-breaking gap?Rarely
07Is management borrowing to buy back stock at a premium multiple?Yes

Two or more red answers: size smaller, write the exit rule before buying, and track the regulator's calendar as closely as the earnings calendar.

Beyond FICO

Where this pattern could show up

Payment networks (MA, V)

The moat is mostly the network itself, which passes check 01. But card fees are a political target, so checks 02 and 05 deserve a look before paying up for them.

Mostly passes

AI power producers

Rising electricity bills tied to data-center demand are becoming a political issue, and grid operators have capped capacity-auction prices before. That is check 02: growth from price on a bill voters see.

Watch closely

The best moats survive a rule change. Own the ones customers would choose anyway.
